DARREN CULLEN / SER: DOCUMENTED HISTORY

The real-world record behind the Graffiti Kings name: interviews, public commissions, exhibitions, media and the later digital pivot.

A history that can be checked

Darren Cullen is the London graffiti writer known as SER and the founder of the Graffiti Kings collective. This page concentrates on reporting, interview testimony and dated public records. It is a companion to the Darren Cullen / SER profile, which also explores the project’s fictional W81 setting.

Statements reported in Cullen’s own interviews are labelled as his account. An article about a commission is different evidence from a company advert, an auction estimate is not a sale result, and fictional lore is not a historical record. The connected-media directory maps the underlying external sources.

1983 onward: Croydon, writing and the SER name

In a long interview published by FAD Magazine in August 2012, Cullen traced his first attempts at writing names to Croydon in 1983. He recalled friends returning from New York with new nicknames and the later influence of the film Style Wars on their lettering and spray-paint experiments. He explained that SER initially stood for South East Rockers, a loose crew designation that he eventually used as his own writer name. Read the FAD interview.

The same account describes the Croydon writers’ bench, the late-1980s and early-1990s scene, and work with crews including MJS and TCN. A later interview for Page Common adds his recollection of studying graffiti lettering from Subway Art at Mitcham Road library. These are first-person memories, not independently dated logs of each piece. Page Common interview.

From Mitcham youth workshops to railway stations

Cullen described an unusual entry into community work: police directed young people to graffiti workshops at a Mitcham youth club before those sessions were actually running. He was then asked to teach. As the sessions became popular, he brought other artists in to help, while councils in Croydon, Sutton and Lambeth sought similar activity. This expansion of paid youth work was an important step towards the Graffiti Kings collective. FAD: detailed account.

According to Cullen, a railway manager approached the group about involving young writers in a legal mural on the outside of Wallington station. Cullen recalled further work at stations, including Morden, and said Connex employed him on railway projects before he consulted for Railtrack and Thameslink. In his account, the programme covered approximately 15 stations and helped attract local-authority attention. Those claims should be presented as Cullen’s recollections unless supported by additional railway or council records. Page Common interview.

The significance is not that a graffiti style was suddenly invented by an advertising company. It is that a youth-led practice became a paid route for community mural work and, eventually, a wider network of artists working across public and private commissions.

Graffiti Kings: commissions and public work

Graffiti Kings developed as a working group of artists rather than a single studio painter. Cullen described it as covering graffiti, illustration, animation, film, music and events. The collective worked across murals, events and brand projects; individual claimed clients should be attributed to the reporting or portfolio that names them. Read Cullen’s description of the collective.

One independently documented commission was the vivid makeover of an old London black cab for ITV London Tonight’s 2012 Olympic coverage. ITV’s article describes a vehicle decorated by Graffiti Kings in Camden, used by presenter Ben Scotchbrook to report from London during the Games. This is evidence of an ITV-associated art commission, not proof that every Graffiti Kings project was commissioned by the Olympic organising committee. ITV: London Tonight launch Olympic cab.

In the same period, national reporting identified an Adidas football-boot mural as another commission involving Cullen. The Guardian, July 2012.

The 2012 Olympics: work and policing controversy

July 2012 produced two distinct kinds of records: Graffiti Kings’ commercial work around the Olympics and coverage of police restrictions imposed on several graffiti artists before the Games. The Guardian, The Independent and VICE reported Cullen’s arrest and subsequent bail conditions, together with his objections to the allegations and the timing. At the time of those reports, allegations were under investigation; an arrest is not a conviction. These articles do not establish a later court outcome. Guardian report; Independent report; VICE interview.

This contrast — officially commissioned spray-paint work on one side, restrictive policing of graffiti culture on the other — became part of the contemporary debate over who was permitted to make public art, where and under what conditions.

2016: Two documented works at auction

Ewbank’s auction on 26 February 2016 included two separate works catalogued to SER (Darren Cullen) of Graffiti Kings, lots 740 and 741. Both were described as large framed spray-paint works on board, each measuring 118 × 78 cm and each estimated at £800–£1,200. The auction results list both as unsold. These are documentary catalogue records, not evidence of realised auction prices or current market values. Ewbank’s 2016 auction catalogue and results.

2019: brand commentary and international media

A 2019 Bloomberg article, republished by The Jakarta Post, discussed the adoption of graffiti-based advertising by luxury and global brands and quoted Cullen as the founder of Graffiti Kings. His contribution sits in the wider story of graffiti artists moving from contested walls to paid public campaigns. Read the syndicated Bloomberg feature.

In December 2019, EXTREME’s own brand update named Cullen of Graffiti Kings as presenter of Destination Ex, one of four original content series launched that autumn. This is a separately published media-production credit, not a mural commission. EXTREME production update.

Radio, NFTs and the shift into WAX

A Graffiti Kings Radio station is documented in online radio and app directories, including listings for DJ mixes and dance-music programming. These are records of the broadcasting project, not a guarantee that any historic stream or app still operates today. Radio directory; App catalogue.

By August 2022, the blockchain publication Blockleaders was profiling Cullen’s Graffiti Kings experiments on WAX, describing NFT distribution, Telegram-based games and the LFGK token as reported at that date. It supplies a dated independent link between the traditional graffiti collective and its digital projects. The figures and plans in that report are historical snapshots, not a statement of present balances, available rewards or guarantees. Blockleaders, 31 August 2022.

The legacy Graffiti Kings website currently describes the commercial operation as closed and points visitors toward GKniftyHEADS and GraffPUNKS. Those linked channels present their own account of the next chapter. Read those as project-authored material, separate from independent journalism and the wiki’s fictional W81 archive.
